Receipt Rights

Lost the receipt? You can still make a claim

What you need is proof that you bought it from that store, not the paper receipt itself. Nothing in the consumer guarantee sections of the Australian Consumer Law requires the original receipt.

What counts as proof

The law's own examples of a "proof of transaction" (s 100(4)) include:

  • a credit or debit card statement
  • a confirmation or receipt number for a phone or online order
  • a tax invoice, or a handwritten receipt
  • a lay-by agreement
  • a cash register receipt (the usual one)

The proof just needs to show who sold it, when, what it was and the price.

Where to look

  • Your banking app. Search for the store's name. Most banks let you go back several years and download a statement as a PDF.
  • Your email. Search for the store name plus "order", "invoice" or "receipt". Many stores email receipts even for in-store purchases if you gave an email address.
  • Store accounts and loyalty programs. Purchase history in the store's app or membership account often includes in-store purchases.
  • Marketplaces and PayPal keep order histories.
  • Warranty registration emails from the manufacturer often include the purchase date and store.

Paid cash with nothing in writing? It's harder, but not hopeless. Ask the store to look the sale up (many can search by date, product or serial number), and write down when and where you bought it while you still remember.

Next time

For anything that costs $75 or more (before GST), the store has to give you proof of transaction. For smaller purchases you can ask for one, and they have 7 days to provide it (s 100). The paper kind usually fades within a year or two, so take a photo of it on the day.

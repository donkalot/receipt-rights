Receipt Rights

The store says it's out of warranty. Are you stuck?

Often not. The manufacturer's warranty is an extra promise on top of your legal rights. The Australian Consumer Law separately guarantees that goods last a reasonable time, and that guarantee doesn't end when the warranty does.

Why the warranty isn't the limit

  • Anything you buy from a business has to be of acceptable quality, which includes being durable (s 54).
  • "Durable" means lasting as long as a reasonable person would expect, given what the item is, what it cost, and anything the maker or seller said about it (s 54(2) and (3)).
  • A warranty, contract or store policy can't take that away or cut it short (s 64). "12 months' warranty" doesn't mean 12 months of rights.

For example, an $1,800 fridge that stops cooling after two and a half years, with a two-year warranty. Most people expect a fridge to last a lot longer than that, so it probably isn't of acceptable quality, even though the warranty has finished. A $25 kettle that dies at three years is a much weaker case.

How long is "reasonable"?

There's no fixed number in the law. It depends on the kind of item, the price, how it was advertised, and how you used it. It doesn't cover damage you caused or abnormal use (s 54(6)). If you're unsure, ask yourself what you'd have thought if the salesperson had told you "this will break in two years". If you wouldn't have bought it, you have an argument.

What you can ask for

It depends on whether the problem is major or minor.

  • Major failure. For example, a reasonable person wouldn't have bought it knowing about the problem, it's unsafe, or it basically doesn't work and can't easily be fixed (s 260). You choose a refund or a replacement (s 259(3), s 263(4)). A store credit or gift card doesn't count as a refund (s 263(5)).
  • Minor failure. Something that can be fixed reasonably easily. The store chooses whether to repair, replace or refund (s 259(2), s 261), and has to do it within a reasonable time. If it doesn't, you can have it fixed elsewhere and claim the cost, or reject the item (s 259(2)(b)).
  • Knock-on losses. You can also claim losses the failure foreseeably caused, such as the food in a fridge that died (s 259(4)).

To insist on a refund or replacement you need to raise it within the time you'd reasonably expect that kind of fault to show up (s 262). After that, you can still claim a repair or compensation.

What to do

  1. Find your proof of purchase. It doesn't have to be the paper receipt: a bank statement or order email works. Lost the receipt?
  2. Go back to the store, not the manufacturer. Your claim is with the business that sold it to you. If they tell you to contact the manufacturer
  3. Put it in writing. Say what you bought and when, what's wrong, that you're relying on the consumer guarantees, what you want, and give a deadline (14 days is common). Written claims get taken more seriously than counter conversations, and they're evidence if it goes further.
  4. If they still say no, contact your state or territory consumer agency: NSW Fair Trading, Consumer Affairs Victoria, Queensland Office of Fair Trading, Consumer Protection WA, Consumer and Business Services SA, CBOS Tasmania, Access Canberra or NT Consumer Affairs. They can also tell you which small claims tribunal or court handles it.
